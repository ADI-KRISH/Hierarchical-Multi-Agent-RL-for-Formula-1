# FormulaOneRL: Driver Agent and Strategy Agent Architecture

> **Status: design doc, not started.** This is the detailed design for Phase 8's
> two-agent hierarchy — see `docs/roadmap.md`. Earliest realistic entry point is
> after Phase 5 (Driver Agent beats the baseline) or Phase 6 (dashboard), and only
> once the user explicitly greenlights it. Nothing here is implemented; treat this
> as the spec to build from *when* that happens, not a description of current code.
> The condensed version of this same vision lives in `docs/context.md`; this file
> is the detailed version it points to.

## 1. Overview

FormulaOneRL models a Formula 1 race as a hierarchical multi-agent reinforcement learning problem involving two collaborating agents: a **Strategy Agent** operating at a low frequency and a **Driver Agent** operating at a high frequency.

The Strategy Agent represents the role of the race strategist or race engineer. It makes long-horizon decisions concerning pit stops, tyre selection, fuel management, ERS management, and the overall tactical approach of the car.

The Driver Agent represents the driver and is responsible for translating these high-level strategic objectives into continuous, low-level vehicle control through steering, throttle, and braking.

The central idea is that the two agents should not independently control the same aspects of the race. Instead, they operate at different levels of abstraction and different timescales.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Race State}
\rightarrow
\text{Strategy Agent}
\rightarrow
\text{High-Level Goal}
\rightarrow
\text{Driver Agent}
\rightarrow
\text{Vehicle Control}
\rightarrow
\text{Race Environment}
}
$$

This hierarchical interaction is the key research component of FormulaOneRL.

---

## 2. Roles of the Two Agents

### 2.1 Strategy Agent

The Strategy Agent operates at a relatively low frequency, approximately once per lap.

Its objective is to make decisions that improve the overall race outcome rather than optimize an individual corner or individual second of driving.

The Strategy Agent is responsible for:

* Pit-stop timing
* Tyre compound selection
* Tyre-management strategy
* Fuel management
* ERS management
* Overall race pace
* Attack or conservation strategy
* Defensive or aggressive race objectives
* Responding to safety-car situations
* Responding to changing weather and track conditions
* Considering the behaviour and strategy of competing cars

The Strategy Agent therefore answers the question:

> **"What should our overall race strategy be at this point in the race?"**

### 2.2 Driver Agent

The Driver Agent operates at a much higher frequency and is responsible for real-time vehicle control.

Its primary actions are:

* Steering
* Throttle/acceleration
* Braking

The Driver Agent receives the high-level objective generated by the Strategy Agent and determines how to execute that objective on the track.

For example, if the Strategy Agent produces:

> **ATTACK**

the Driver Agent must determine the appropriate steering, throttle and braking behaviour required to achieve a faster lap while maintaining control of the vehicle.

The Driver Agent therefore answers:

> **"Given our current strategy, how should I control the car right now?"**

---

## 3. Hierarchical Relationship

The two agents operate at different timescales.

### Strategy level

The Strategy Agent makes decisions approximately once per lap:

$$
a_t^S = \pi_S(s_t^S)
$$

where \(s_t^S\) represents the race-level state and \(a_t^S\) represents a strategic decision.

### Driver level

The Driver Agent makes many decisions during a single lap:

$$
a_t^D = \pi_D(s_t^D,g_t)
$$

where:

* \(s_t^D\) = local driving state
* \(g_t\) = current high-level strategy goal
* \(a_t^D\) = steering, throttle and braking actions

The strategic decision remains active for a period of time while the Driver Agent continuously executes it.

---

## 4. Strategy Agent State

The Strategy Agent should receive information that describes the overall race rather than detailed instantaneous vehicle-control information.

A possible strategy state is:

$$
s_t^S =
[
R_t,
T_t,
F_t,
E_t,
O_t,
W_t,
C_t
]
$$

where:

* \(R_t\) = race state
* \(T_t\) = tyre state
* \(F_t\) = fuel state
* \(E_t\) = ERS/energy state
* \(O_t\) = opponent state
* \(W_t\) = weather/track state
* \(C_t\) = race-control conditions

### Race State

The Strategy Agent can consider:

* Current lap
* Remaining laps
* Current race position
* Gap to the car ahead
* Gap to the car behind
* Current race time
* Recent lap times

### Tyre State

The Strategy Agent can consider:

* Current tyre compound
* Tyre age
* Estimated tyre degradation
* Current tyre condition
* Expected performance of alternative compounds

### Fuel State

The Strategy Agent can consider:

* Fuel remaining
* Estimated fuel consumption
* Remaining race distance
* Required fuel-saving level

### ERS State

The Strategy Agent can consider:

* Available energy
* Recent energy usage
* Expected recovery
* Deployment requirements

### Opponent State

The Strategy Agent can consider:

* Positions of nearby competitors
* Gaps to competitors
* Their tyre compounds
* Their tyre ages
* Their recent lap times
* Whether competitors have pitted
* Potential changes in competitor strategy

### Environment State

The Strategy Agent can consider:

* Weather
* Track conditions
* Track temperature
* Safety-car status
* Other major race events

The exact observation space should be finalized during simulator design and experimentation.

---

## 5. Strategy Agent Actions

The Strategy Agent can use a discrete action space.

A strategic action can be represented as:

$$
a_t^S =
[
P_t,
\tau_t,
M_t
]
$$

where:

* \(P_t\) = pit decision
* \(\tau_t\) = tyre decision
* \(M_t\) = strategic mode

### Pit Decision

Possible actions:

* PIT
* STAY OUT

### Tyre Decision

Possible choices:

* Soft
* Medium
* Hard

The selected tyre becomes relevant when a pit stop occurs.

### Strategic Mode

The Strategy Agent can communicate a high-level mode such as:

* ATTACK
* NORMAL
* CONSERVE TYRES
* SAVE FUEL
* DEFEND

Additional modes can be introduced depending on the simulator and experimental requirements.

---

## 6. Communication Between Strategy and Driver

The most important interaction between the agents is the transfer of the Strategy Agent's decision to the Driver Agent.

The Strategy Agent should not directly control the steering, throttle or brake.

Instead, its decision is converted into a **high-level strategy mode vector**.

For example:

$$
g_t =
[
p_t,
\tau_t,
f_t,
e_t,
m_t
]
$$

where:

* \(p_t\) = target pace
* \(\tau_t\) = tyre-management objective
* \(f_t\) = fuel-management objective
* \(e_t\) = ERS-management objective
* \(m_t\) = tactical mode

Example:

```text
Strategy Command
-------------------------
Target Pace       : High
Tyre Management   : Low
Fuel Management   : Normal
ERS Management    : Deploy
Tactical Mode     : Attack
```

This vector is added to the Driver Agent's observation.

Therefore:

$$
s_t^D =
[
s_{\text{vehicle}},
s_{\text{track}},
s_{\text{tyre}},
s_{\text{local race}},
g_t
]
$$

The Driver Agent then generates:

$$
a_t^D =
[
\text{steering},
\text{throttle},
\text{brake}
]
$$

This creates a direct and explicit communication pathway from high-level strategy to low-level control.

## 7. Example: Attack Mode

Suppose the Strategy Agent observes:

* The car is behind a competitor
* The gap is small
* Tyres still have sufficient performance
* Fuel is sufficient
* The race situation rewards gaining a position

The Strategy Agent may select:

```text
Mode = ATTACK
Target Pace = HIGH
Tyre Management = LOW
Fuel Management = NORMAL
ERS = DEPLOY
```

The Driver Agent receives this information.

It does not simply execute a predefined "attack" command. Instead, it learns how to translate the objective into vehicle control.

For example:

```text
Track Section
      ↓
Driver observes corner
      ↓
Determine braking point
      ↓
Determine steering
      ↓
Determine throttle
      ↓
Maintain high pace
```

Thus, the Strategy Agent determines **the objective**, while the Driver Agent determines **the control actions required to achieve it**.

---

## 8. Example: Tyre Conservation

Suppose the Strategy Agent predicts that the current tyres must last several additional laps.

It can generate:

```text
Mode = CONSERVE
Target Pace = MODERATE
Tyre Management = HIGH
Fuel Management = NORMAL
ERS = CONSERVE
```

The Driver Agent then learns to modify its driving behaviour accordingly.

The resulting behaviour may include:

* Smoother acceleration
* Reduced wheel slip
* Less aggressive cornering
* More controlled braking
* Reduced tyre stress

The important point is that the Driver Agent does not need a separate manually programmed driving policy for every strategy.

The same learned driving policy is conditioned on the high-level strategic objective.

---

## 9. Example: Fuel Saving

If fuel becomes limited, the Strategy Agent may select:

```text
Mode = FUEL SAVE
Target Pace = MODERATE
Tyre Management = NORMAL
Fuel Management = HIGH
ERS = CONSERVE
```

The Driver Agent then modifies its control policy according to the strategy.

The objective is no longer simply:

> "Complete the lap as quickly as possible."

Instead, it becomes:

> "Complete the lap efficiently while satisfying the current race strategy."

This creates an important trade-off between immediate lap time and long-term race performance.

---

## 10. Example: Pit Stop Decision

Consider a car approaching a strategic decision point.

The Strategy Agent observes:

```text
Current Position       : P6
Tyre Age               : High
Tyre Degradation       : Increasing
Fuel                   : Sufficient
Car Ahead               : Recently Pitted
Safety Car              : Active
```

The Strategy Agent may select:

```text
PIT
New Tyre = Hard
Mode = CONSERVE
```

The pit decision is executed by the race environment.

The Driver Agent does not need to decide which tyre should be fitted.

Instead, it receives the appropriate driving objective, such as:

```text
Prepare for Pit
```

After the pit stop, the race state changes.

The Strategy Agent then receives the new state and makes another strategic decision.

This creates a closed-loop system:

$$
\text{Strategy}
\rightarrow
\text{Driver}
\rightarrow
\text{Race}
\rightarrow
\text{New State}
\rightarrow
\text{Strategy}
$$

---

## 11. Safety-Car and Weather Events

The system should also be capable of responding to unexpected events.

For example:

$$
\text{Normal Racing}
\rightarrow
\text{Safety Car}
\rightarrow
\text{Strategy Re-evaluation}
$$

The Strategy Agent receives the new race state and can reconsider:

* Whether to pit
* Whether to change tyre strategy
* Whether to conserve tyres
* Whether to alter fuel/ERS strategy
* Whether to change the tactical mode

Similarly, changing weather can alter the strategy.

The proposal identifies safety-car and weather events as important perturbations for evaluating the overall system.

---

## 12. Separation of Responsibilities

The architecture should maintain a clear division between the two agents.

| Responsibility                  | Strategy Agent | Driver Agent |
| ------------------------------- | -------------: | -----------: |
| Pit timing                      |              ✓ |              |
| Tyre selection                  |              ✓ |              |
| Long-term tyre strategy         |              ✓ |              |
| Fuel strategy                   |              ✓ |              |
| ERS strategy                    |              ✓ |              |
| Attack/conserve objective       |              ✓ |              |
| Race-level positioning strategy |              ✓ |              |
| Steering                        |                |            ✓ |
| Throttle                        |                |            ✓ |
| Braking                         |                |            ✓ |
| Corner-by-corner control        |                |            ✓ |
| Immediate vehicle control       |                |            ✓ |

This separation prevents the two agents from competing over the same action space.

## 13. Reward Design

The two agents should have rewards that reflect their different responsibilities.

### Strategy Reward

The Strategy Agent should be evaluated primarily on race-level outcomes.

A possible reward is:

$$
R_S =
w_1R_{\text{position}}
-w_2R_{\text{race time}}
-w_3R_{\text{pit loss}}
-w_4R_{\text{tyre degradation}}
-w_5R_{\text{DNF}}
$$

The exact weighting should be determined experimentally.

The strategy reward should encourage decisions that improve the overall race result rather than simply maximizing one lap.

### Driver Reward

The Driver Agent should receive more immediate feedback.

A possible reward is:

$$
R_D =
w_1R_{\text{progress}}
+w_2R_{\text{lap time}}
-w_3R_{\text{collision}}
-w_4R_{\text{track violation}}
-w_5R_{\text{excess tyre stress}}
$$

The Driver Agent therefore learns efficient and stable vehicle control while following the strategic objective.

## 14. Overall Race Interaction

The complete race can be represented as:

```text
                 RACE ENVIRONMENT
                        │
                        ▼
                 Race-Level State
                        │
                        ▼
                STRATEGY AGENT
                Low Frequency
                        │
           ┌────────────┼─────────────┐
           │            │             │
        Pit Plan     Tyre Plan    Race Mode
           │            │             │
           └────────────┼─────────────┘
                        │
                        ▼
                Strategy Mode Vector
                        │
                        ▼
                  DRIVER AGENT
                 High Frequency
                        │
             ┌──────────┼──────────┐
             │          │          │
          Steering   Throttle    Brake
             │          │          │
             └──────────┼──────────┘
                        ▼
                 Vehicle Dynamics
                        │
                        ▼
                   Race State
                        │
                        └──────────────►
                         Strategy Agent
```

This architecture allows strategic decisions and vehicle-control decisions to be learned at their natural timescales.

## 15. Training Strategy

A staged-learning approach:

### Stage 1 — Driver Agent

Train the Driver Agent using a predefined or rule-based strategy.

The objective is to first develop a competent driver.

```text
Rule-Based Strategy
        ↓
Driver Agent
        ↓
Vehicle
```

### Stage 2 — Strategy Agent

Once a competent Driver Agent exists, train the Strategy Agent on top of it.

```text
Strategy Agent
       ↓
Competent Driver Agent
       ↓
Race Environment
```

This allows the Strategy Agent to learn race-level decisions without simultaneously having to learn basic vehicle control.

### Stage 3 — Optional Joint Fine-Tuning

After both agents become competent, they can optionally be fine-tuned together.

```text
Strategy Agent
       ↕
Driver Agent
       ↕
Race Environment
```

This staged approach is intended to reduce instability compared with training both agents from scratch simultaneously.

**Mapping onto the MVP roadmap:** Stage 1 here is already the MVP (Phases 1-5) — a
competent Driver Agent that beats a rule-based baseline. Stage 2 (the Strategy
Agent) and Stage 3 (joint fine-tuning) are Phase 8 work and haven't started.

## 16. Research Objective

The central research question becomes:

> **Can a hierarchical multi-agent reinforcement learning system learn to coordinate long-horizon race strategy with high-frequency vehicle control and achieve better complete-race performance than matched rule-based strategies?**

The project can evaluate:

* Lap time
* Race completion time
* Final position
* Tyre degradation
* Pit-stop efficiency
* Driver consistency
* Response to safety-car events
* Response to weather changes
* Strategy decisions
* Stability and sample efficiency of staged versus joint training

Key success criteria: the Driver Agent's improvement over a fixed-line driver, the
Strategy Agent's improvement over a fixed-window strategy, and appropriate response
to safety-car and weather perturbations.

---

## 17. Key Design Principle

The most important principle of FormulaOneRL is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Strategy Agent decides WHAT to optimize}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{Driver Agent decides HOW to execute it}
}
$$

For example:

```text
Strategy Agent:
"Attack for the next lap."

             ↓

Driver Agent:
"Given the track, vehicle state and
attack objective, what steering,
braking and throttle actions should
I take at this instant?"

             ↓

Vehicle:
Executes the learned control policy.
```

This separation allows FormulaOneRL to model the fundamental distinction between
**race strategy** and **driving control**, while explicitly studying how decisions
at the two different timescales influence one another.

## 18. Prerequisites this design assumes (not yet true of the codebase)

This design was written for a car/track model with a full pose (`x, y, heading`)
and opponent cars on track. Neither exists yet — the current `DriverEnv` (Phase 2)
is a single point-mass car with no steering-to-heading model and no opponents.
Before Stage 1 above can literally be "the MVP," at least these gaps need
addressing (scoped as their own Phase 8 sub-tasks, not bundled into one giant
rewrite):

* Opponent cars, collision handling, and relative-position observations (for
  `O_t`, overtaking, and defending) — currently entirely absent.
* Tyre degradation, fuel load, and ERS state as simulated, evolving quantities
  (currently `CarParams` is static for a whole run) — needed for `T_t`, `F_t`, `E_t`.
* A race-control layer (safety car, VSC, weather) — currently doesn't exist.
* The Driver Agent's observation needs the strategy mode vector `g_t` appended
  — a small, mechanical change to `DriverEnv`'s observation space once the rest
  exists.
